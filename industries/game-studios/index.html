WHY GAME MARKETING IS DIFFERENT

Four Things That Make Mobile Game Marketing Its Own Discipline

Video game marketing is not app marketing with better artwork on it. The economics, the discovery mechanics and the shape of a release are all different.

Free-to-play economics set the ceiling on CPI

In a free-to-play title a small share of players carries the revenue for everyone else, so the payback curve is long, uneven and specific to your game. Mobile game marketing has to start from that curve: what an install is allowed to cost is a consequence of monetisation design, not a media-buying preference.

1

Store discovery is a ranking problem, not an ad problem

A large part of game installs still comes from browsing and searching inside the App Store and Google Play. Keywords, category placement, icon, screenshots and the first seconds of the preview video decide whether paid traffic converts at all. Buying more installs into a weak listing just raises the price of every install.

2

Creative burnout is the real spend driver

Game creatives fatigue faster than almost any other vertical. A concept that carried the campaign last month stops converting, frequency climbs, and CPI drifts up while nothing about the targeting has changed. Video game marketing at scale is a production problem: enough new concepts, tested on schedule, retired before they drag the account.

3

A launch is a curve, not a date

Soft launch in a few markets to read retention and early monetisation, then a global release, then live operations that keep returning to the same audience with events and updates. Each phase needs a different channel mix and a different definition of success, and the plan has to be written that way from the start.

WHY OUR APPROACH WORKS

What a Video Game Marketing Company Has to Get Right

Three things that separate a working growth loop from an invoice for media buying.

Unit Economics First

Payback, not install counts

Every campaign is briefed against a payback window agreed before launch. Installs are an input, not a result: the numbers that decide whether we scale or stop are cost per install measured against what those cohorts return, by market and by creative.

  • Target CPI derived from your own payback curve
  • ROAS read by cohort, not by calendar month
  • Scale and stop rules written down in advance

Creative as a Production Line

Volume, tested, retired on schedule

Game creatives decay, so the programme plans for replacement instead of reacting to it. A defined number of new concepts per cycle, a test structure that reads results before the budget is spent, and retirement rules so fatigued concepts stop dragging the account.

  • Planned concept volume per cycle
  • Structured creative testing, not sequential guesses
  • Creator footage reused as performance creative

STILL HAVE QUESTIONS?

Questions about mobile game marketing

What it costs, how it is planned, and where the boundaries of our scope sit.

How much does mobile game marketing cost?

The audit that opens an engagement starts at a few thousand dollars and covers the store listing, cohort data and creative library. After that the work is priced as fixed-price scopes rather than a blended retainer, so you can start with one piece and add the rest later. Media spend is separate and stays yours. On the mobile game marketing budget question, the honest answer is that the ceiling is set by your payback curve, not by us: we would rather agree a target cost per install and a payback window first and size the budget from there.

What does a mobile game marketing strategy actually look like?

It starts with monetisation, not with channels. We read what a player is worth over the payback window you care about, derive the cost per install that maths allows, and only then choose the mix. From there a mobile game marketing plan has three phases: fix the store listing so traffic converts, buy installs in the channels that reach your audience at that price, and keep retention and creative moving so the price does not drift back up. The best mobile game marketing campaigns we have seen were not clever ideas — they were ordinary ideas attached to a cost ceiling that was worked out in advance.

Do you run user acquisition for mobile games?

Yes. Mobile game user acquisition is scoped, described and priced on the app user acquisition service page, and this page links down to it rather than repeating it. In practice that means Apple Search Ads, Google App Campaigns and paid social, structured around a target cost per install and a payback window instead of a monthly spend target. The packages sit on the App User Acquisition service page, and the individual pieces of work are the Apple Search Ads and Google App Campaigns scopes, all of them linked from the cards below and from the related services at the foot of this page.

Do you do ASO for mobile games?

Yes, and it is usually the first thing we do. Store optimisation for a game is different from a utility app: category competition is brutal, the icon and preview video carry most of the conversion, and keyword sets shift with every featured slot and seasonal event. The discipline lives on the App Store Optimisation service page, the fixed-price work is the ASO Optimisation scope, and store listing localisation is a separate scope for when you open a new market. All three are linked from this page rather than restated on it.

What does the marketing mix look like across channels?

For most mobile titles the mobile game marketing mix is roughly: store optimisation as the foundation, Apple Search Ads and Google App Campaigns as the dependable volume, paid social for creative-led scale, and creator or community work feeding both awareness and the creative library. The exact split is a consequence of your genre and your payback window — a mid-core title with a long payback curve can afford channels a casual puzzle game cannot, and the mix is revisited every cycle against cohort performance.

How do you structure a marketing campaign for a mobile game?

Each mobile game marketing campaign gets a single job: a soft-launch campaign exists to read retention and early monetisation cheaply, a global launch campaign exists to buy volume at a known price, and a live-operations campaign exists to bring existing players back around an event. Mixing those jobs into one campaign is the most common mistake we see, because the success criteria contradict each other and nobody can tell afterwards which part worked.

Do you have a mobile game marketing case study we can read?

Not a published one. Our public case studies are from other verticals, and we will not dress one of them up as a game case. What we can point to is the named specialist on this account: Muhammad Hijazi Siddiqui has run store optimisation and paid acquisition for mobile games and apps with results including 100%+ tROAS and millions of installs. On a first call he will walk through that work directly, and we will scope the audit against your numbers rather than against a slide.

What should we keep in-house and what do you take on?

Studios ask this as marketing your mobile game yourself versus hiring out, but it is rarely all or nothing. Keep everything close to the product: community voice, event calendar, in-game economy decisions. Hand over the parts that need daily attention and specialist tooling: store optimisation, campaign management, creative testing and cohort reporting, with the store-page and lifecycle half of that covered by the App Conversion and Retention service. A common split is that you produce the game footage and we turn it into tested performance creative.

Do you do SEO for a game website or a studio site?

Yes, as a supporting channel rather than the main one. Search demand around a specific title is mostly brand demand that follows the marketing rather than creating it, so a game website earns its keep as a conversion and press surface: a fast site, clean technical foundations, press kit, and pages that rank for your own title name before someone else does. If you want that half of the work, it runs through the SEO service and the keyword research, technical SEO and on-page scopes behind it.

Which kinds of games do you take on?

Games as products: premium titles sold once, free-to-play games monetised through in-app purchases, subscription titles, and ad-monetised games. That list is the whole of our scope and we keep it that way on purpose — it is the boundary that keeps a studio account in good standing with the ad platforms we buy on, and it keeps the store optimisation and acquisition work we do transferable from one client to the next.

Do you work with PC and console titles?

Partly, and we would rather be precise about it. Our store optimisation service is built for the App Store and Google Play, so we do not sell store-page work for PC or console storefronts today. Everything that is platform-independent still applies: performance creative and trailers, press and creator programmes, community work, analytics and attribution. If your title is cross-platform, we take the mobile half plus the platform-independent parts, and we say so in the proposal rather than after signature.

Can you run a launch in several markets at once?

Yes, and it is the usual shape of a global release. Each market gets its own store listing treatment, its own creative variants and its own bid strategy, because install cost, competitive pressure and player expectations are not transferable between markets. Work is delivered by native specialists per language rather than by translating one market's assets, and the reporting layer above them uses the same definitions everywhere so the markets can actually be compared.
