WHY CONTENT PROGRAMMES STALL

Three Failures That Stall a Content Strategy

Three failures show up in almost every website content audit we run. None of them are solved by producing more articles.

Volume without a thesis

A team publishes steadily and still cannot say what the blog is for. Without a topic thesis, meaning the short list of subjects you intend to own and the reason a buyer should believe you on them, every brief is argued from scratch and the archive reads as unrelated posts. This is where content strategy and content marketing part company: strategy decides what the programme is about before anyone writes a word.

1

No owner behind a topic

Blog content strategy fails quietly when a cluster belongs to everyone. Slots get filled by whoever has capacity that week, depth never accumulates, and the pieces that would actually move a buying decision are the ones nobody has time for. A calendar with a named owner and a fixed format per slot changes what gets published.

2

Nothing is ever retired

Content programmes almost never include a decision to stop. Pages that stopped earning years ago still split rankings with newer work, consume crawl budget and mislead the people who read them. Deciding to merge or delete is as much a strategic output as deciding to publish.

CONTENT STRATEGY FAQ

Common Questions About Content Strategy

Straight answers about scope, pricing and delivery.

How much does a content audit cost?

A 50-page website content audit costs $500, 200 pages cost $1,000 and 500 pages cost $1,800. Libraries above 2,000 pages are scoped individually. Published prices cover the English core and one primary country; each additional language adds 60% of the item base price and each additional country adds 40%.

How long does a content audit take?

The S tier (50 pages) takes 5 business days, the M tier (200 pages) 8 business days and the L tier (500 pages) 12 business days. Strategy development runs 2 to 3 weeks after the audit is signed off, and the editorial calendar is handed over within a week of the strategy.

What is included in a content strategy?

A topic thesis and audience definition per market, topic clusters mapped to funnel stage and destination page, format and cadence decisions, a measurement plan naming the metric each cluster is judged on, and a costed production plan. The dated editorial calendar with owners, formats and briefs is the separate Editorial Calendar task, from $400.

What is the difference between content strategy and content marketing?

Content strategy decides what to publish, for whom, in what order and how success is measured. Content marketing is the ongoing programme that produces, distributes and promotes it. This scope is the strategy half: one bounded engagement ending in a plan. If you are looking for the full programme, including production and distribution, start from our content marketing service page.

Do you run content gap analysis and competitor content analysis?

Yes, on our SEO research scope. Both are priced and delivered as tasks there, from $400, so the same work never appears twice in one order. They pair well with an audit: the audit tells you what to do with the pages you have, gap and competitor analysis tell you which pages you are missing.

Can we run the content audit ourselves?

You can, and the method is not secret: export every URL, join traffic and conversion data, add the last-updated date, then force one decision per row. What teams usually lack is the willingness to delete. Buying the audit buys an outside opinion that has no stake in keeping a page alive.

Do I need a content strategist or a copywriter?

A strategist decides which pages should exist and how they will be judged. A copywriter writes them. If you already know what to publish and only need the words, go straight to the writing scopes. If you are publishing steadily and cannot show what it earns, start with the audit here.

How often should you audit your content?

Once a year for most libraries, or after any event that changes the picture: a migration, a rebrand, a new market, or a sharp traffic drop. Fast-moving libraries publishing more than twenty pieces a month benefit from a lighter half-year pass on the newest cohort only.
