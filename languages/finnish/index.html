Fifteen Cases, Compounds and Keyword Research

Finnish stacks endings onto stems across fifteen grammatical cases and writes compounds as single words, so one English phrase becomes one long Finnish token and one Finnish noun appears in dozens of search-visible forms. Research has to be done in the language, on the forms people actually type, or the campaign targets words that do not exist.

Where Machine Translation Breaks in Finnish

Machine output into Finnish is usable for gist and unreliable for marketing: it splits compounds that must be joined, misses the colon before an ending on abbreviations, and inserts demonstratives where Finnish simply has no articles. TEXT

Finnish the language, Finland the market

This page is about the language. Finnish is the first language of 5,288,825 people in Finland, about 94% of the country's 5,626,410 residents, and the dataset behind this page tracks 5,772,497 speakers across three markets. If the decision in front of you is geographic (whether to enter at all, how Helsinki and Uusimaa concentrate commercial demand, what a Finnish media plan looks like, how Finland fits a wider EU rollout), that belongs on the Finland market page. The two are kept apart so neither has to answer both questions badly.

What makes Finnish different is structural, not stylistic. It is a Finno-Ugric language in the Uralic family: not related to Swedish, Danish, Norwegian, German, English or Russian, however long it has sat next to them. It is agglutinative, so meaning is built by stacking suffixes onto a stem across fifteen grammatical cases, and the stem itself changes as they attach, which is why kauppa, a shop, becomes kaupassa, in the shop. It writes compounds as one word, so search engine optimisation is hakukoneoptimointi and customer service is asiakaspalvelu. It has no articles, no grammatical gender and no future tense, and vowel harmony decides which variant of an ending a word takes. None of that is decoration. It decides what a keyword looks like, how many forms demand splits across, and which sentences a machine can and cannot produce.

The practical consequence is that keyword tooling built on English behaves badly here. A three-word English query maps to a single Finnish token, so a long-tail phrase arrives looking like a head term and n-gram clustering copied from an English playbook mis-groups it. A single noun is search-visible in the nominative, the genitive, the partitive and a dozen locative forms, and volume is reported against whichever form was typed into the tool, so the same demand can be counted three times or missed entirely. A literal Finnish rendering of an English head term is very often a zero-volume phrase. Research therefore starts from Finnish search data and Finnish forms, not from a translated list.

What transfers from the neighbours is the market, not the language. Nordic channel mix, buyer behaviour, Google's dominance, the EU consent and data framework and the general comfort with English all carry over from a Swedish or Danish programme. The keyword set carries over not at all. Finnish is close to Estonian and to nothing else useful, and even that closeness is a trap rather than a shortcut: the two share structure and a lot of false friends, and raamattu in Finnish is the Bible while raamat in Estonian is a book. Finnish content does not serve an Estonian audience, and Estonian content does not serve a Finnish one.

Whether Finnish is worth it at all is a fair question and the honest answer is: for some categories, not all. About 70% of Finland reads English comfortably, and several B2B, SaaS and technical categories run there in English with no local version and no visible penalty. Finnish wins consumer categories, regulated and trust-sensitive ones, public-facing content and the local long tail, where reading a translated page is noticed and held against you. English-language demand for Finnish-language services is itself thin, in the low hundreds of searches a month, so this is not a page that argues volume. The usual sequence is to localize the pages that already convert, measure them against the English versions, and extend Finnish only where the lift shows up.

We are not a Helsinki agency competing for Finnish clients. We are an outsourced, productized marketing service for companies based outside the Finnish-speaking world. Being straight about staffing: there is no named Finnish specialist on our team today. Finnish copy is produced and reviewed by native Finnish linguists sourced through our Alconost localization heritage, and search, paid and analytics execution is run by the same in-house specialists who run every other language, briefed against Finnish research. The Finnish specialist role is open, and the positions page carries that ask.

FAQ

Finnish SEO and localization: common questions

Is it worth publishing in Finnish at all?

For some categories, not all, and the honest answer depends on who buys from you. Finnish is the first language of about 94% of Finland's 5,626,410 residents, and roughly 70% of the country also reads English comfortably. That combination means several B2B, SaaS and technical categories run in Finland in English with no local version and no visible penalty, while consumer, regulated, trust-sensitive and locally searched categories lose measurably without Finnish. The usual sequence is to localize the pages that already convert, measure them against the English versions, and extend only where the lift is visible.

Can we reuse our Swedish or Nordic keyword list for Finland?

No, and this is the single most expensive assumption people bring to Finland. Finnish is a Finno-Ugric language in the Uralic family, unrelated to Swedish, Danish, Norwegian, German or English, so a Nordic programme that treats Scandinavian coverage as Finnish coverage simply does not reach the market. What does transfer from a Swedish or Danish programme is the market layer: channel mix, buyer behaviour, the EU consent and data framework, the general comfort with English. The keyword set transfers nothing at all and has to be rebuilt in Finnish.

What do fifteen cases and compound words do to keyword research?

They break the arithmetic. Finnish is agglutinative: endings stack onto a stem across fifteen grammatical cases, and the stem changes as they attach, so kauppa, a shop, becomes kaupassa, in the shop, and a single noun is search-visible in a dozen forms that a tool counts separately. Finnish also writes compounds as one word, so search engine optimisation is hakukoneoptimointi, which means a three-word English phrase arrives as a single token and English-trained clustering mis-groups it. The practical rule is that research runs on Finnish forms from Finnish data, and a literal Finnish rendering of an English head term is very often a phrase with no volume behind it.

Does machine translation work well for Finnish?

It is usable for gist and unreliable for marketing, and the failures are specific rather than vague. Machine output splits compounds that Finnish requires to be written as one word, which is the error native readers flag first. It mishandles the colon that goes before a case ending on an abbreviation, so EU:ssa and SEO:n come back wrong. It inserts demonstratives where Finnish has no articles to translate. It flattens the partitive and accusative distinction that tells a Finnish reader whether an action is ongoing or finished. And it carries English superlatives straight into a register that treats them as overclaim. We do offer machine translation with post-editing where volume genuinely justifies it, such as catalogue or support content, and we call it MTPE rather than presenting it as native writing.

Finnish or Swedish for the Finnish market?

Usually Finnish first, with Swedish as a deliberate second decision rather than an afterthought. Finnish is read by 94% of Finland, English by 70% and Swedish by 44%, and Swedish is the country's second national language, concentrated in Ostrobothnia, parts of the south coast and the Åland Islands. Finland Swedish is also not Sweden Swedish, so copy lifted from a Stockholm campaign reads as imported. Which language carries which category is a targeting call made before anything is written, and the versions then need hreflang and a clean internal-linking split so they do not bid for the same query.

Does Finnish content reach Estonia or Finnish speakers in Sweden?

Not as a publishing strategy, no. The dataset behind this page counts 250,696 Finnish speakers in Estonia and 232,976 in Sweden, where Finnish is a recognised regional minority language, and both figures reflect comprehension rather than a searching, converting audience of that size. Estonian is Finnish's closest relative and the resemblance is a trap: the two share structure and a long list of false friends, where raamattu in Finnish is the Bible while raamat in Estonian is a book. Reaching Estonia means Estonian-language work, not Finnish content pointed at a neighbouring country.

Who actually writes the Finnish, and do you have a named Finnish specialist?

There is no named Finnish specialist on our team page today, and we would rather say that than imply coverage we have not built. Finnish copy is produced and reviewed by native Finnish linguists sourced through our Alconost localization heritage, which covers 20+ years of professional localization and translation across 100+ languages. Search, paid and analytics execution is run by the same in-house specialists who run every other language, briefed against Finnish research and reviewed natively before anything goes live. The Finnish specialist role is open and listed on the positions page.

How much does Finnish translation cost per word, and what does a Finnish copywriter charge?

We do not bill Finnish work per word or per hour. Everything ships as a fixed-price productized scope with a defined deliverable, so a Finnish landing-page set, a Finnish content batch or a Finnish keyword research scope has a published price before it starts and the price does not move because a text turned out longer than expected. Per-word pricing suits translation vendors; it fits Finnish especially badly, because agglutination makes Finnish word counts far lower than the English source while the research and adaptation behind the words are most of the actual effort.
